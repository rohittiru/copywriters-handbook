# Writing Public Relations Materials

PR materials must work for two audiences: the media (editors, reporters, producers) who decide whether to cover you, and the end reader/viewer who sees the resulting coverage. The key difference from advertising: you don't control the final message. You provide the raw material and make it irresistible to use.

## The Press Release

**Purpose:** Announce newsworthy information to media outlets. A press release is NOT an ad. It's a news story written in journalistic style that gives editors a reason to cover you.

**Structure:**
1. **FOR IMMEDIATE RELEASE** (or embargo date) at the top
2. **Headline** — factual, news-oriented, not promotional. State the news clearly.
3. **Subhead** — optional; adds a second dimension to the story
4. **Dateline** — city and date
5. **Lead paragraph** — answers Who, What, When, Where, Why, and How in the first 1–2 sentences. The lead must stand alone as a complete story.
6. **Body paragraphs** — expand on the news in descending order of importance (inverted pyramid). Include quotes from key people.
7. **Boilerplate** — standard company description paragraph (2–3 sentences)
8. **Contact information** — name, phone, email of the PR contact
9. **###** or **-30-** to signal the end

**What makes a press release newsworthy:**
- New product launch or significant product improvement
- Major partnership, acquisition, or milestone
- Research findings or survey results
- Awards or industry recognition
- Event announcement
- Expert commentary on a trending topic
- Unusual human interest angle

**What kills a press release:**
- Thinly disguised advertising copy
- Hype words: revolutionary, groundbreaking, world-class, cutting-edge
- No actual news — just a company wanting attention
- Burying the news under company background
- Missing contact information

## Feature Articles / Bylined Articles
- Written as educational content, not promotion
- Establish the author (your client's executive) as a thought leader
- Published in trade magazines, industry sites, or business publications
- The "sell" is subtle: the author's bio mentions their company and role
- Structure: engaging lead → problem definition → expert analysis → practical advice → conclusion

## Case Studies
- Tell the story of a specific customer's success with your product
- Structure: Challenge → Solution → Results
- Use real numbers: "reduced costs by 37%" not "significantly reduced costs"
- Include direct quotes from the customer
- Make the customer the hero, not your product

## Media Kits
Include:
- Company backgrounder (history, mission, key facts)
- Executive bios with headshots
- Product/service fact sheets
- Recent press releases
- Key statistics and milestones
- Customer testimonials or case study summaries
- High-resolution logos and product images
- Contact information

## PR Writing Style
- Write like a journalist, not a copywriter — objective tone, factual, no hype
- Use AP style for press releases
- Keep sentences and paragraphs short
- Attribute claims and statements to named sources
- Include specific data, not vague superlatives
- Make it easy for editors to use your material by writing clean, publishable copy

## Feature Article Types

When ghostwriting articles for publication, there are four main formats:

**Case histories:** Tell the story of how a company solved a problem. Structure: situation, problem, solution, results. Most editors love case histories because they give readers real-world examples they can learn from.

**How-to articles:** Provide step-by-step instructions on a specific topic. The most popular article format. Example: "How to Remove Tough Stains" (with your client credited as the expert source).

**Issue articles:** Discuss a controversial or timely industry issue from your client's expert perspective. Positions the client as a thought leader.

**News articles:** Report on a new development, product, trend, or discovery. Must contain genuine news, not just a sales pitch.

## Query Letters

To get articles published, pitch editors with a query letter BEFORE writing the full article:
- State the proposed topic and angle
- Explain why the magazine's readers would care
- Briefly describe your (or your client's) credentials on the topic
- Keep it to one page
- Include a P.S. with credentials or a relevant writing sample
- Most publications now accept digital queries via email or online submission form
- A positive response means "send a manuscript" — not a promise to publish
- If rejected, send the query to other publications

## Writing Speeches and Presentations

Average speaking rate: 120 words per minute. A 20-minute speech = ~2,400 words.

Lunch/dinner talks should be about 20 minutes. No speech should exceed an hour.

**Speech writing tips:**
- **Find out what the speaker wants to say.** Interview the client deeply. Sometimes they haven't clarified their own position until you probe.
- **Know your audience.** Tailor the talk to the specific group's interests, knowledge level, and concerns.
- **Write a strong opening.** You have 60 seconds to hook the audience or lose them.
- **Don't try to cover too much.** Speeches communicate ideas, opinions, and emotions — not large bodies of facts (print is better for that).
- **Write in a conversational tone.** Speeches are spoken, not read. Write for the ear.
- **Keep it simple.** One big idea per speech, supported by a few key points.
- **Use visuals sparingly.** Slides should support the speech, not replace it.

**Slide/PowerPoint rules (when slides are required):**
- Don't show slides continuously — use a blank dark slide between visuals so attention returns to the speaker
- Use a visual only when it communicates better than words (show the picture of the thing; don't put the word "Quality" on screen)
- One major visual per slide; avoid cluttered process diagrams; readable from the back row
- Keywords, not sentences — slides are the framework, the speaker is the script; keywords permit large type
- Simple backgrounds (black on white when in doubt); skip decorative clip art
- Design the talk to survive a tech failure — visuals are an enhancement, never a crutch
- **Prepare handouts.** For detailed information the audience can take away and reference later.
- **Pick a catchy title.** The title is the "headline" of the speech — it's what appears in the conference program and determines whether people attend.

## Newsletter Story Ideas Checklist

When creating a company newsletter, draw from this list:
News, explanatory articles ("how it works"), product stories, case histories, background information, how-to-solve-a-problem articles, technical tips for using the product, general how-to information and advice, do's and don'ts, industry updates, employee news and profiles, community relations news, Q&A columns, letters from readers, book reviews, calendar of events, market data and statistics, awards and recognitions, guest columns from industry experts.
