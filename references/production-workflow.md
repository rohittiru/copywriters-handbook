# Getting Copy Written, Designed, and Produced

This reference covers the workflow of managing copy — from commissioning it through revision to final production. Essential for anyone running the process, not just writing.

## Options for Getting Copy Written

**Option 1: Write it yourself.** Best when: the product is highly technical, you'd spend more time briefing a writer than writing it, or you want it in your specific voice. Copy-competent clients often handle some pieces themselves and outsource others.

**Option 2: Outsource to freelancers.** Advantages: specialized skill, higher response rates from experienced copywriters, fresh perspective. A "little bit better" copy can mean hundreds of thousands in additional revenue when conversion rate lifts even a few percentage points.

**Option 3: Insource to staff writers.** Advantages: dedicated focus on your projects, easy access to internal subject matter experts, team collaboration on ideas and reviews. Disadvantage: may lack the specialized direct-response expertise of top freelancers.

**Option 4: AI/software tools.** AI can generate subject lines, draft initial copy, and compare variations using data. Best used as a tool that supports human copywriters, not a full replacement — especially for complex, emotional, or high-stakes copy.

## How to Hire a Copywriter

**Finding writers:** Referrals are best. Ask ad agencies, PR firms, printers, designers, and communications managers at other firms. Search online for writers with portfolios in your industry.

**What to look for:**
- Is their own marketing material well-written? (If they can't sell themselves, they can't sell your product.)
- Do you like their sample work? These are their BEST pieces — if they don't impress you, more samples won't help.
- Do they have experience in your industry or related fields?
- Do they provide fee information upfront? Professionals are transparent about pricing.
- What related experience do they have? (Books, articles, speaking, teaching.)
- What's your gut feeling? Trust your instincts about professionalism and fit.

**Key hiring rules:**
- Choose a writer whose style matches yours — a creative agency won't convert you to splashy image ads, and you won't convince them to write hard-sell copy. Save everyone the frustration.
- Hire in your league — not every assignment needs a top-tier specialist. Match the writer's level (and fee) to the job's complexity.
- Choose industry experience over exact product experience — a travel copywriter can learn your cruise line, but a fashion writer will struggle.

## Working With Copywriters

**Discuss fees upfront.** No point proceeding if the budget doesn't match. Get ballpark estimates by project type. If they charge hourly/daily, ask how long they estimate your project will take.

**Discuss deadlines upfront.** Top copywriters are booked weeks in advance. State your timeline early. Ask about their typical turnaround for projects of your size.

**Discuss revisions upfront.** How many rounds are included in the base fee? What constitutes a "revision" vs. a "new assignment"? Is there a time limit for requesting revisions? Get this clear BEFORE work begins.

**Provide complete background information.** Send all available materials: previous ads, brochures, annual reports, competitor info, market research, product specs, customer testimonials, and anything else relevant. Use the research checklist from the Getting Ready to Write process (see SKILL.md).

**Respect the professional.** Treat copywriters like you'd treat your lawyer or accountant. Stand back and let them do their job. The relationship works best when there's mutual respect and clear communication.

## The 25-50-25 Principle of Copy Revisions

When a client requests changes to your copy:
- **~25% of changes improve the copy** — make these gratefully and thank the client
- **~50% of changes make no difference either way** — make these without question or quibble
- **~25% of changes weaken the copy** — voice your reasons why, but if the client disagrees, acquiesce pleasantly

This principle prevents ego-driven battles while protecting the work where it matters most. Pick your fights — only push back on the changes that will genuinely hurt results.

## How to Review and Approve Copy

Most copy disputes stem from subjective opinions ("I don't like it") rather than concrete criteria. Use the Copy Quality Checklist (see SKILL.md) as your review framework.

**Productive review process:**
1. Read the copy from the customer's perspective, not your own
2. Evaluate against the agreed-upon objective — does it accomplish the goal?
3. Check it against the brief — does it address all required points?
4. Apply the Copy Quality Checklist systematically
5. Be specific in feedback — "The headline doesn't promise a clear benefit" is useful; "I don't like it" is not
6. Consolidate feedback from all reviewers before sending to the writer — don't send contradictory notes from multiple people
7. Allow the writer to push back — they may have good reasons for their choices

**Common review mistakes:**
- Rewriting copy to match your personal style rather than evaluating effectiveness
- Letting personal taste override proven sales principles
- Having too many people review and edit (committee copy is always weaker)
- Insisting on changes that weaken the selling message for aesthetic reasons
- Comparing your copy to competitors' "image" ads when you need direct-response results

## Copy Design and Production

**Working with designers:**
- The headline and visual must work together as one concept — discuss this with the designer before either of you starts working
- Don't let design override readability — pretty layouts that suppress copy effectiveness are a waste of money
- Specify the hierarchy: what should the eye see first, second, third?
- Body copy must be readable: sufficient type size, line spacing, and contrast
- Don't reverse large blocks of copy (white text on dark background) — it kills readability

**Layout principles for sales materials:**
- Eye flow should follow a logical path: visual → headline → body → CTA
- Use graphic devices (subheads, bullets, bold, indentation) to break up long copy
- Leave enough white space — cramming more words in smaller type does not increase readership
- Captions under photos are read 2x more than body copy — always include them and make them sell
- The CTA (order form, button, coupon, phone number) should be prominent and easy to find

**Production checklist:**
- [ ] Copy has been proofread for errors (spelling, grammar, facts, prices, URLs, phone numbers)
- [ ] Legal/compliance review completed (claims substantiated, disclaimers included)
- [ ] Contact information is correct and complete
- [ ] CTA is clear and all response mechanisms work (links, forms, phone numbers)
- [ ] Brand guidelines followed (logo usage, color palette, fonts)
- [ ] Mobile responsiveness tested (for digital materials)
- [ ] File formats delivered as specified by printer/platform
- [ ] Tracking mechanisms in place (unique URLs, codes, phone numbers for attribution)

## Testing and Optimization

- Test one variable at a time: headline, offer, price, format, list, or creative
- The mailing list (or traffic source) has the biggest impact on results — even great copy can't overcome a bad list
- Always test against a control (your current best-performing piece)
- Run tests for statistical significance before declaring winners
- Keep a "swipe file" of winning promotions for future inspiration
- Document what works and what doesn't — institutional knowledge compounds over time
