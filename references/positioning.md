# Positioning: The Battle for the Prospect's Mind

Based on Al Ries and Jack Trout's framework as applied in The Copywriter's Handbook. Positioning is what the advertising does for the product in the prospect's mind. It answers: "When the prospect thinks of this product category, where does YOUR product sit?"

---

## The Core Principle

Positioning does NOT replace features, benefits, and sales arguments — it COMPLEMENTS them. It establishes the product's identity and competitive angle BEFORE you start selling the specifics.

Your copy must do two jobs: (1) make the consumer think about the product in the right way (positioning), AND (2) persuade the consumer to buy it (selling). You can't skip either.

---

## Positioning Examples from the Book

- **Coors:** Positioned as a fresh, pure beer made from the water of the Rocky Mountains (purity + natural origin)
- **Heineken:** Positioned as a premium beer "crafted by connoisseurs" (sophistication + exclusivity)
- **Coca-Cola:** Positioned through brand dominance — you can get cola from a dozen makers, but if you want a Coke, only Coca-Cola has it
- **Intel Pentium:** Achieved brand dominance through massive investment — when people think "processor," they think Intel
- **Sarah Jessica Parker for Stella Artois:** Positioned as a sophisticated alternative to cocktails (lifestyle positioning via celebrity association)
- **Alex Trebek for Colonial Penn:** Positioned as life insurance for intelligent, discerning people (credibility positioning via spokesperson)

## How to Determine Your Positioning

### Step 1: Define the Category
What product category are you competing in? Don't define it too narrowly (just your exact product type) or too broadly (all products that solve any problem). Find the category the prospect naturally puts you in.

### Step 2: Identify the Competition
Who are you positioned AGAINST? What do prospects currently use instead of your product? What would they buy if your product didn't exist?

### Step 3: Find Your Angle
How is your product different from competitors in a way the prospect cares about? Options:

**Position by attribute:** Own a specific feature or characteristic (Volvo = safety, BMW = driving performance)

**Position by price/value:** The affordable option, the premium option, or the best-value option

**Position by use/application:** The best product for a specific use case (like Blackmer pump — not the best pump generally, but provably the best for viscous fluids and slurries)

**Position by user:** The product for a specific type of person (Inc. magazine = for entrepreneurs, not corporate executives)

**Position against a competitor:** Directly contrast yourself with the market leader (Avis: "We're #2, so we try harder")

**Position by category:** Create or redefine the category entirely (Dollar Shave Club didn't position as a razor — they positioned as a convenience subscription)

## Positioning and Copy: How It Flows Through

Once you've defined the positioning, it should inform everything:

- **Headline:** Reflects the positioning angle, not just a generic benefit
- **Tone:** Matches the position (premium = sophisticated; value = practical; innovative = forward-looking)
- **Proof points:** Selected to reinforce the position (if positioned as reliable, lead with uptime data and longevity; if positioned as innovative, lead with patents and new technology)
- **Visuals:** Support the positioning (premium products need premium design; rugged products need rugged imagery)
- **Audience targeting:** The position determines WHO you're talking to and who you're deliberately excluding

---

## Positioning vs. USP

These are related but different:

- **USP** is the specific, concrete claim that differentiates you: "Melts in your mouth, not in your hand"
- **Positioning** is the broader strategic space you own in the mind: "The candy you can carry without making a mess"

The USP supports the positioning. The positioning provides the strategic context for the USP.

---

## When Positioning Is Especially Critical

- Entering a crowded market with established competitors
- Launching a product that's genuinely similar to existing options
- Repositioning an existing product that's lost market relevance
- Targeting a niche segment within a broad category
- Justifying a premium price point
- Writing brand-level copy (homepage, about page, company brochure)
