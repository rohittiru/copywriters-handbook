# Writing Brochures, Catalogs, and PDF Sales Materials

Brochures are "leave-behind" or "send-ahead" materials that support the sales process. Unlike ads (which must grab attention from uninterested readers), brochures are read by people who have already expressed some interest. This means you can go deeper on details — but you still must sell, not just inform.

## Types of Brochures
- **Sales brochure:** Sells a specific product or service; follows the motivating sequence
- **Capabilities brochure:** Sells the company as a whole; establishes credibility and range
- **Product catalog:** Multiple products organized for easy browsing and ordering
- **Technical data sheet:** Detailed specs for engineers and technical buyers
- **Annual report:** Sells the company to investors and stakeholders

## Brochure Structure

**Cover (front):**
- Headline with a strong benefit — NOT just the product name
- Compelling visual
- Company logo
- The cover is the "ad" for the brochure itself — if it doesn't hook, nothing inside gets read

**Inside copy:**
1. Restate and expand the cover's promise
2. Describe the problem or need the product addresses
3. Present the product as the solution
4. Features and benefits (use a two-column format or bullets)
5. Proof elements: testimonials, case studies, specs, awards, certifications
6. Application examples or use cases
7. Product photos, diagrams, charts

**Back cover:**
- Company contact information
- CTA (call, visit website, request a demo)
- Brief company description or boilerplate
- Locations/dealer network if applicable

## Catalog Copy
- Each product listing needs a mini-ad: headline, brief description, key benefit, price, and order info
- Group products logically (by category, application, or price)
- Use consistent format across all listings so readers can compare quickly
- Include an index and table of contents for easy navigation
- Feature "best sellers" or "editor's picks" to guide indecisive buyers
- Cross-sell with "customers also bought" or "works well with" references

## Writing Tips for Brochures
- Don't just list features — translate every feature into a customer benefit
- Organize information with clear subheads so readers can find what matters to them
- Use captions on every photo — captions are read 2x more than body copy
- Include a powerful testimonial on every spread if possible
- Make the brochure self-contained — it may be read without a salesperson present
- Consider the reader's technical level and write accordingly
- For capabilities brochures: lead with client results, not company history
- Every brochure should answer: What is it? What does it do for me? Why should I buy from you? What do I do next?

## PDF Sales Materials
Digital brochures follow the same principles with added considerations:
- Include clickable links to landing pages, demo videos, and order forms
- Keep file size reasonable for email attachment (under 5MB)
- Design for screen reading — larger type, more white space
- Use bookmarks and a clickable table of contents for longer documents
- Consider interactive elements: embedded video, fillable forms

## The 11 Tips for Writing Better Sales Brochures

1. **Know where the brochure fits in the buying process.** Is it a leave-behind after a meeting? A response to an inquiry? A point-of-sale piece? A sales aid during a pitch? The application determines the content.

2. **Know whether the brochure stands alone or is supported by other materials.** A brochure accompanied by a salesperson can be sparser; one mailed cold must do the full selling job on its own.

3. **Know your audience.** A brochure for engineers needs specs and technical data. A brochure for consumers needs benefits and lifestyle imagery. Never write in a vacuum.

4. **Put a strong selling message on the front cover.** A surprising number of brochure covers contain no headline — just a logo. This wastes a valuable selling opportunity. The cover is the "ad" for the brochure itself.

5. **Give complete information.** Give as much as it takes to get the prospect to take the next step. People read brochures like novels — cover first, quick peek at back, then thumb through.

6. **Organize your selling points.** Write the brochure as a series of short sections, each with its own subhead. This lets scanners get the gist at a glance.

7. **Divide the brochure into short, easy-to-read sections.** Headers and subheads guide the reader. Short sections are easier to write AND read.

8. **Use hardworking visuals.** Every image should support the selling message. Put a caption on every photo — captions are read 2x more than body copy.

9. **Find the next step in the buying process — and tell the reader to take it.** Include a clear CTA: call, visit the website, request a demo, contact a dealer.

10. **Don't overlook the obvious.** Include basic info: company name, address, phone, website, social profiles. It sounds obvious but it's frequently missing.

11. **Make the brochure worth keeping.** Include useful reference information (charts, tips, checklists, specs) that gives the reader a reason to file it rather than toss it.

## Other Sales Literature Types
Less common formats you may be asked to write — same selling principles, different containers:
- **Annual reports** — capabilities-brochure content plus financial performance; polished, credibility-first tone
- **Flyers** — one unfolded sheet, printed one or two sides; cheap reach for handouts, trade shows, local posting
- **Broadsides** — flyers folded for mailing; good for announcing sales or news to an existing customer list
- **Invoice stuffers** — small promos sized for a #10 envelope, mailed with bills or statements; they ride free with routine correspondence
- **Circulars** — 4–8 page color ad sheets distributed by mail, package/newspaper insert, or hand; usually coupon-driven retail
- **Pamphlets/booklets** — like brochures but carrying useful general information rather than a specific product pitch; a soft-sell credibility builder
- **White papers** — sell like a brochure but read like an objective article; must contain genuine how-to value that helps the reader solve a problem or justify a decision (full treatment in `content-marketing.md`)
