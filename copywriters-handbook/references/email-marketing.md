# Writing Email Marketing

Two types of marketing emails: **Solo emails** (promoting a single product/offer to a list) and **E-newsletters/e-zines** (online newsletters that build relationships and can contain product ads).

Strategy: Build an email list by offering a free newsletter subscription, then send both e-zines (with embedded product mentions) and solo promotional emails.

## 15 Rules for Effective Email Copy

**1. FROM and SUBJECT lines are everything.** The SUBJECT line is your envelope teaser — it must arouse curiosity and compel opens without being so blatantly promotional it triggers spam filters or reader disgust.

FROM line options:
- **List owner** — when renting a list and the owner has a trusted relationship
- **Your company** — when you're a recognized brand or market leader
- **Your name** — for personal messages or when company isn't well-known
- **Team name** (e.g., "The Adobe Team") — for collaborative/product efforts

**2. The word FREE boosts response** despite spam filter risks. Test it. In most cases, FREE still lifts response rates. But be aware some ISPs filter it.

**3. Lead with a killer headline or opening sentence.** Pretend you're writing envelope teaser copy. Get a terrific benefit right up front. Example subject: "6 shocking financial forecasts."

**4. First paragraph = mini-version of entire message.** State the offer and provide an immediate response link. This serves readers with short attention spans who give your email only 2 seconds.

**5. After the lead, expand with full details.** Features, benefits, proof, and supporting information for readers who need more before deciding.

**6. Repeat the offer and response link at the close,** just as in a direct-mail letter. But the offer should ALSO appear at the very beginning.

**7. Limit to 3 click-through links.** The majority of clicks come from the first two links. Exception: e-newsletters with multiple distinct items, each with its own link.

**8. Use wide margins.** Limit lines to 55–60 characters. Insert manual line breaks to prevent ugly wraps. Set margins at 20 and 80.

**9. Easy on the ALL CAPS.** Use sparingly. All caps reads as SHOUTING online and can trigger spam filters.

**10. Short is usually better for emails** (unlike direct mail where "the more you tell, the more you sell"). Email readers are sorting through dozens of messages and won't stick around long. See "Long vs. Short Email Copy" below for the quantified rule.

**11. Get key points across quickly.** The main benefit and deal should be visible on the first screen. Those who need more can click through to the landing page.

**12. Tone: helpful, friendly, informative, educational.** Not hard-sell or promotional. "Information is the gold in cyberspace." Be specific about HOW you're the best — don't just claim "quality" or "the best."

**13. Always include an opt-out mechanism.** State your intention to respect privacy. Make removal easy ("Reply with REMOVE in subject line" or unsubscribe link). This is both legally required and good practice.

**14. Test subject lines relentlessly.** Small changes in subject lines can dramatically affect open rates. A/B test every send.

**15. Personalize when possible.** Use the recipient's name and any known data points to make the email feel written specifically for them.

## Subject Line Best Practices

Apply the 4 U's formula (Urgent, Unique, Ultra-specific, Useful) to every subject line. Rate each dimension 1–4.

Weak: "Free Special Report" (not urgent, not unique, not specific)
Strong: "Free report reveals how to cut training costs 90% with e-learning" (specific, useful, unique angle)

Avoid: subject lines that sound like spam ("AMAZING OFFER!!!", "You won't BELIEVE this")
Use: curiosity, news, specific benefits, numbers, questions

## Spam Filter Avoidance
- Avoid excessive use of FREE, GUARANTEED, ACT NOW, $$$ in subject lines
- Don't use all caps in subject lines
- Keep HTML clean and balanced (text-to-image ratio matters)
- Use a recognizable FROM name
- Include your physical mailing address
- Maintain clean lists — remove bounces and unsubscribes promptly
- Send from authenticated domains (SPF, DKIM, DMARC)

## Long vs. Short Email Copy
The "short copy wins online" advice needs quantifying — three rules resolve the debate:
1. **Calibrate the terms.** "Short" online means 3–4 paragraphs. "Long" email means several screens — roughly a one-page letter, nowhere near a 4-page direct-mail letter. Long email can absolutely work; a 4-page letter pasted into an email cannot.
2. **The half-length rule.** Online text should run about half the length of the equivalent print copy — a rough starting estimate, not a law.
3. **The email is only half the message.** The persuasion is split across two parts: the email opens the sale and earns the click; the landing page carries the full argument and closes. Never try to complete the whole sale inside the email — write the email as the first half of a relay.
Buying online doesn't change the persuasion process: if the prospect needs the facts to decide (typical for high-ticket, subscription, and info products), the facts must exist somewhere in the chain — push the length to the landing page, not the email.

## Email Sequence Strategy
For lead nurture sequences, each email should:
1. Provide genuine value (tip, insight, story, resource)
2. Build credibility and trust incrementally
3. Gradually introduce the product/offer
4. Include a soft CTA in early emails, harder CTA in later ones
5. Address objections one at a time across the sequence

## E-Newsletter Best Practices
- Provide useful, educational content — not just promotions
- Keep a consistent sending schedule
- Lead with the most compelling content item
- Use a "letter from the editor" personal tone
- Include 3–5 short content items with links to full articles
- Soft-sell product mentions woven naturally into content
- Always include a clear CTA for one featured offer
