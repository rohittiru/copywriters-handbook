# Writing Print Advertisements

Print ads (magazine, newspaper, trade journal) must compete with editorial content and dozens of other ads for the reader's attention. The headline and visual do 80% of the work.

## Anatomy of a Print Ad
1. **Visual** — photo, illustration, or graphic that works WITH the headline
2. **Headline** — the most important copy element (see headline principles in SKILL.md)
3. **Body copy** — expands on the headline's promise with features, benefits, and proof
4. **Logo/company name** — brand identification
5. **Contact info/CTA** — how to respond (URL, phone, coupon, QR code)

## Ad Layout Approaches

**Editorial style (advertorial):** Designed to look like a magazine article. Uses a strong headline, columns of text, and informational tone. Often outperforms traditional ad layouts because readers engage with it as content, not advertising.

**Picture-caption:** Series of photos with captions underneath. Each caption sells a different feature/benefit. Readers naturally read captions, so this format gets high readership.

**Problem-solution:** Visual shows the problem; headline and copy present the solution. Works well for B2B and industrial products.

**Testimonial:** Customer photo + testimonial in quotes. Simple, credible, effective.

**Product-as-hero:** Large product photo dominates. Best for products with strong visual appeal or name recognition.

## Body Copy Principles
- Fulfill the headline's promise immediately — don't make the reader wait
- Long copy can work in print (especially trade/B2B) if every sentence sells
- Use subheads every few paragraphs to re-engage scanners
- Include specifics: model numbers, dimensions, performance data, pricing
- Close with a clear CTA and response mechanism

## Ad Sizes and Copy Length
- Full page: room for long copy if warranted by the product
- Half page: concise copy focused on one main benefit; headline should be 8–12 words
- Quarter page: headline + brief copy + CTA; every word counts
- Classified/small space: direct headline, compressed benefit statement, response info

## Slogans and Taglines
A slogan sums up the central message of a campaign or the nature of the company, usually beneath the logo.
- **Rule: form follows function.** Use a slogan only if the product's key selling proposition can be captured in one pithy, memorable line. Never force-fit one — an artificial slogan detracts from the ad.
- **The two failure modes:** too narrow (pigeonholes you in one product category you may outgrow) vs. too broad (so general it loses all meaning). Test the line against both.
- Slogans only pay off with repetition — memorable phrasing repeated to the same audience over years, not months. If the client won't commit long-term, skip it.
- A great slogan doubles as positioning: it should state the benefit or character of the brand, not just sound clever.

## Response Mechanisms
- Coupons (clip and mail or bring to store)
- QR codes linking to landing pages
- Toll-free numbers
- Website URLs
- "Mention this ad" for in-store offers

## B2B/Industrial Ad Specifics
- Feature technical specs that engineers and buyers need
- Lead with the application or problem solved, not just the product feature
- Include case studies or performance data
- Offer free technical resources (white papers, spec sheets, application guides)
- Don't assume "dull" means ineffective — "Handling Sulfuric Acid" is fascinating to a chemical engineer battling corrosion
