# Generational Marketing

Different generations have fundamentally different formative experiences, communication preferences, technology relationships, and buying behaviors. Advertising that targets specific generations has become commonplace and extremely effective — because the generations differ more sharply today than in decades past, driven by accelerating technology, economic volatility, and geopolitical instability.

---

## Generational Profiles

### Matures (Born before 1945)
- **Formative experiences:** WWII, rationing, fixed gender roles, nuclear families, big-band music
- **Aspiration:** Home ownership
- **Technology attitude:** Largely disengaged
- **Career attitude:** Jobs are for life
- **Communication media:** Formal letter
- **Communication preference:** Face-to-face meetings
- **Financial decision-making:** Face-to-face meetings
- **Signature product:** Automobile
- **Copy implications:** Formal tone, respect for authority and institutions, emphasize security and tradition, print-friendly formats

### Baby Boomers (1945–1960)
- **Formative experiences:** Cold War, postwar boom, "Swinging Sixties," Apollo moon landings, Woodstock, family-oriented, rise of the teenager
- **Aspiration:** Job security
- **Technology attitude:** Early IT adapters
- **Career attitude:** Organizational careers defined by employers
- **Communication media:** Telephone
- **Communication preference:** Face-to-face ideally, but telephone or email if required
- **Financial decision-making:** Face-to-face ideally, increasingly going online
- **Signature product:** Television
- **Copy implications:** Value quality and brand loyalty, respond to nostalgia and legacy, comfortable with long-form content, transitioning to digital but still value print

### Generation X (1961–1980)
- **Formative experiences:** End of Cold War, fall of Berlin Wall, Reagan/Thatcher era, Live Aid, introduction of the PC, early mobile technology, latchkey kids, rising divorce rates
- **Aspiration:** Work-life balance
- **Technology attitude:** Digital immigrants (adopted technology as adults)
- **Career attitude:** Early "portfolio" careers — loyal to profession, not necessarily to employer
- **Communication media:** Email and text message
- **Communication preference:** Text message or email
- **Financial decision-making:** Online preferred, face-to-face if time permits
- **Signature product:** Personal computer
- **Copy implications:** Skeptical of hype, value authenticity and directness, research before buying, respond to practical benefits over emotional appeals, comfortable with email marketing

### Generation Y / Millennials (1981–1995)
- **Formative experiences:** 9/11, terrorism, social media, invasion of Iraq, reality TV, Google Earth, Glastonbury
- **Aspiration:** Freedom and flexibility
- **Technology attitude:** Digital natives
- **Career attitude:** Work "with" organizations, not "for" them; entrepreneurial
- **Communication media:** Text or social media
- **Communication preference:** Online and mobile (text messaging)
- **Financial decision-making:** Face-to-face (surprisingly)
- **Signature product:** Tablet/smartphone
- **Copy implications:** Short attention spans, mobile-first content, value experiences over things, social proof matters enormously (reviews, ratings, peer recommendations), distrust traditional advertising, respond to transparency and purpose-driven brands

### Generation Z (Born after 1995)
- **Formative experiences:** Economic downturn, global warming, global focus, mobile devices, energy crisis, Arab Spring, WikiLeaks, cloud computing
- **Aspiration:** Security and stability
- **Technology attitude:** "Tech holics" — entirely dependent on IT, limited grasp of alternatives
- **Career attitude:** Career multitaskers — move seamlessly between organizations and "pop-up" businesses
- **Communication media:** Handheld (or integrated into clothing) communication devices
- **Communication preference:** FaceTime, video
- **Financial decision-making:** Will be digital/crowd-sourced
- **Signature product:** Google Glass, 3-D printing, driverless cars, nanocomputing
- **Copy implications:** Video-first content, ultra-short form, visual over text, authenticity is non-negotiable (they detect manufactured messaging instantly), influencer endorsements over traditional celebrity, social causes and values matter in purchase decisions

## How to Use This for Copy

### Step 1: Identify the Dominant Generation in Your Audience
Most products have a primary generational target. A retirement planning service targets Boomers. A new social app targets Gen Z. A productivity SaaS might target Gen X and Millennials. Know which generation(s) you're writing to.

### Step 2: Match Format to Preference
- **Boomers:** Long-form content works. Email marketing effective. Print still viable.
- **Gen X:** Email and web. Practical, fact-based copy. Skip the hype.
- **Millennials:** Mobile-first. Short, visual, social-proof heavy. Purpose matters.
- **Gen Z:** Video-first. Ultra-short. Authentic or invisible. Influencer > brand voice.

### Step 3: Match Tone to Values
- **Boomers:** Professional, authoritative, quality-focused
- **Gen X:** Direct, no-BS, practical
- **Millennials:** Conversational, transparent, purpose-driven
- **Gen Z:** Casual, visual, values-forward, peer-to-peer

### Step 4: Match Proof to Trust Framework
- **Boomers trust:** Expert endorsements, brand reputation, company credentials
- **Gen X trusts:** Data, comparisons, track record, ROI
- **Millennials trust:** Peer reviews, social proof, user-generated content, ratings
- **Gen Z trusts:** Influencer endorsements, video testimonials, brand values alignment

---

## Caution
These are generalizations — useful for initial targeting, but individuals within any generation vary widely. Use generational insights as a starting point, then refine based on specific audience research and BDF analysis.
