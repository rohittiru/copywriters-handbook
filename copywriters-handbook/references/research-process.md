# The Research & Preparation Process (Adapted for AI-Assisted Copywriting)

The original process assumes a human copywriter who can call clients, visit factories, and conduct phone interviews. As an LLM, you compensate by being more thorough in questioning the user, analyzing uploaded materials, and conducting web research. The principles are identical — only the information-gathering mechanics change.

---

## The Core Principle

90% of what you need to write great copy comes from thorough research. Copywriting failures are almost always research failures — the writer didn't know enough about the product, the audience, or the objective. As an LLM, you must be MORE disciplined about gathering information upfront, because you can't rely on intuition from years of industry experience.

---

## Step 1: Analyze Everything the User Has Already Provided

Before asking a single question, extract maximum value from what's already in context:

- **Uploaded files:** Previous ads, brochures, landing pages, sales decks, product specs, customer reviews, competitor materials. Read them thoroughly and extract features, benefits, tone, positioning, and proof points.
- **Conversation history:** The user may have described their product, audience, or goals earlier. Don't re-ask what they've already told you.
- **User memories:** What do you already know about their business, clients, industry, and past projects?
- **The request itself:** Even a brief request ("write me a landing page for my course") contains clues about the product type, format, and implied audience.

---

## Step 2: Conduct Web Research (Your Substitute for Industry Knowledge)

Where a human copywriter draws on years of industry experience, you compensate with targeted research:

- **The client's website and social profiles:** Study their current positioning, messaging, tone, and proof points. What's working? What's missing?
- **Competitor websites and ads:** How are competitors positioning? What claims are they making? What language are they using? Where are the gaps you can exploit for a USP?
- **Industry context:** What's happening in this market right now? What trends, fears, or opportunities are top-of-mind for the target audience?
- **Customer reviews and forums:** What language do actual customers use to describe their problems and desires? (This is gold for copy — mirror their exact words back to them.)
- **Existing content and case studies:** What proof points, testimonials, or results already exist that you can weave into the copy?

Do this research BEFORE asking the user questions. Come to the conversation informed, not ignorant. The user should feel like you already understand their business.

---

## Step 3: The Discovery Brief (Your Substitute for Client Interviews)

In the book, copywriters conduct phone interviews with clients and SMEs. As an LLM, you conduct a structured discovery conversation with the user. But do it efficiently — consolidate your questions, don't ask one at a time across 10 messages.

### Questions About the Product
Ask only what you couldn't learn from uploaded materials or web research:
- What are the key features and benefits? (Or confirm: "From what I've read, the main features are X, Y, Z — is that right? What am I missing?")
- What makes this different from competitors? What can you claim that they can't or don't?
- If nothing is truly unique, what underpublicized benefit can we own?
- What problems does this solve? What does life look like before vs. after?
- What proof exists? (Testimonials, case studies, data, certifications, track record)
- Price, guarantee, and offer details?
- What objections do prospects typically raise?

### Questions About the Audience
- Who is the ideal buyer? (Demographics, psychographics, role, industry)
- What keeps them up at night? What are they afraid of?
- What do they desire most? What change do they want?
- What have they already tried that didn't work?
- How sophisticated are they about this product category? (Naive vs. informed buyer)
- What would make them say "this is exactly what I've been looking for"?

### The BDF Analysis (Build This Together)
Walk the user through mapping their audience's:
- **Beliefs:** What does the audience believe about themselves, their industry, and the problem?
- **Desires:** What do they want? What goals, outcomes, or lifestyle changes?
- **Feelings:** How do they feel right now? Frustrated, fearful, hopeful, skeptical, overwhelmed?

Frame it as: "Let me understand your audience on a deeper level. If I were sitting across from your ideal customer right now, what would they tell me about..."

The BDF directly shapes the emotional angle of the copy.

### Questions About the Objective
- What is the ONE action you want the reader to take?
- Where does this piece sit in the sales funnel? (Cold traffic? Warm leads? Existing customers?)
- Is this one-step (sell directly) or two-step (generate a lead)?
- What's the desired conversion metric?
- Are there compliance restrictions? (Platform ad policies, industry regulations, legal disclaimers)

### How to Ask Efficiently
Don't interrogate the user with 30 questions. Instead:
1. Lead with what you ALREADY know from research and context
2. Confirm or correct your understanding
3. Ask only the gaps — the things you genuinely couldn't figure out
4. Group related questions together
5. If the user gives brief answers, follow up on the most critical gaps only

Example: "From your website and the materials you shared, here's what I understand: [summary]. The main things I'd need from you to write strong copy are: [3-5 specific questions]. Can you fill me in on those?"

## Step 4: Build the Features/Benefits List

Before writing a single word of copy, create a two-column list:

| Feature | Benefit |
|---|---|
| [What the product IS or HAS] | [What this DOES for the buyer] |

For every feature, ask: "So what? Why does the buyer care?" Keep asking until you reach an emotional or practical payoff.

Then rank the benefits:
1. Which is the strongest? (This becomes the headline theme)
2. Which is the most unique? (This might be the USP)
3. Which addresses the audience's biggest pain point? (This might be the emotional hook)

---

## Step 5: Identify the USP

From the features/benefits list and competitive research, determine:
- Is there a genuinely unique feature competitors lack? → Lead with it
- If not, is there an underpublicized benefit no competitor has claimed? → Own it
- If not, can you reposition around a specific application or audience? (Like the Blackmer pump example — not better in general, but provably best for specific use cases)

The USP must pass three tests: (1) it promises a specific benefit, (2) competitors can't or don't offer it, (3) it's important enough to move buyers.

---

## Step 6: Create the Copy Platform (Outline)

Before drafting, present a brief outline to the user for approval:
- **Working headline** (your strongest option)
- **Theme/angle** (one sentence: what's the core promise and emotional hook?)
- **Key sales points** (in the order you'll present them)
- **Proof elements** (what testimonials, data, or case studies you'll use)
- **CTA** (the specific action you'll ask for)
- **Tone** (conversational, authoritative, urgent, empathetic, etc.)

This prevents wasted effort. If the user disagrees with the angle, you find out BEFORE writing 2,000 words.

---

## Step 7: Write in Three Stages

### Stage 1: Get It All Down
Let the copy flow. Follow the motivating sequence. Don't edit while writing. If you're writing a landing page, sales letter, or email — imagine you're writing to a friend about a product you're genuinely excited about.

### Stage 2: Edit
- Cut unnecessary words ruthlessly
- Replace big words with simple ones
- Break long sentences into short ones
- Strengthen the headline (try 6-8 variations)
- Add specifics where claims feel vague
- Add proof where claims feel unsupported
- Check that every section drives toward the CTA
- Read it aloud (mentally) — does it sound like a real person talking?

### Stage 3: Quality Check
Run the Copy Quality Checklist from SKILL.md. Verify all facts, prices, URLs, and response mechanisms. Check that the copy fulfills the headline's promise.

## Step 8: The Idea Generation Toolkit (When You're Stuck)

1. **Restate the problem clearly** — sometimes you're stuck because you haven't defined what you're solving
2. **Review the features/benefits list** — a headline might be buried in a benefit you overlooked
3. **Consult the swipe file** — match the product to one of the 38 headline approaches
4. **Try the word association technique** — list 15-20 words related to the product, then mix and match them into headlines
5. **Flip the perspective** — write from the customer's voice, not the advertiser's
6. **Apply the BDF** — if the copy isn't connecting, you may be writing to the wrong belief, desire, or feeling
7. **Try "How to" as a starter** — Bly says "if you begin with 'how to,' you can't write a bad headline"
8. **Use false logic** — find a true fact that, when framed skillfully, leads to the conclusion you want
9. **Add a secondary promise** — if the big claim is too hard to believe, pair it with a smaller, credible backup
